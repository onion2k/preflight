How to Use Preflight

You built a website with an AI. Launching it is a different job from building it, and it is the part nobody shows you — so hand that job to the AI as well. The checklist knows how to verify 51 of its 73 checks; your assistant does the looking. Pick your app below and it will tell you exactly what to do.

What the checklist actually is

      
73 things worth checking before a website goes live, grouped by the kind of
      problem they prevent.

      
None of it is about whether your site is good. It is about the specific, boring failures
      that are invisible from the inside and expensive from the outside: a page that tells Google
      not to list you, a contact form that quietly sends nothing, a broken link in the one place
      people click, a copyright year stuck in the past.

      
You will not need all 73. Plenty will not apply to you, and the checklist says so where
      that is likely. The point is to decide about each one rather than discover it later.

    


    

      
Start here if you have twenty minutes

      
Eight items are marked blocker. They are the ones that are hard to undo, or that
      do damage quietly while you think everything is fine. In rough order of how often they go
      wrong on a site built quickly:

      
    
        
  1. Placeholder text. Search your live site for words like lorem,
        TODO, example.com and Coming soon. AI-built sites are full of them,
        and they are invisible until someone else reads the page.
    2. 
        
  2. The robots file. One line in a file called robots.txt can tell
        every search engine to ignore your entire site. It is the single most common launch
        mistake, and you cannot see it by looking at your pages.
    3. 
        
  3. The 404 page. Type nonsense after your domain name. You should get a proper
        "not found" page with a way back — not a blank screen, and not your homepage pretending
        nothing is wrong.
    4. 
        
  4. Secrets in the code. If you pasted an API key, password or token anywhere
        during building, assume it is public. Check, and change it if it was.
    5. 
        
  5. The privacy page. If your site collects anything at all — a form, analytics, an
        embedded video — it needs a page saying so, honestly.
    6. 
        
  6. Who owns the domain. It should be registered to you, with auto-renew on and a
        working email address on the account. Domains lapse silently.
    7. 
        
  7. Security headers. A handful of settings that tell browsers how to protect
        people on your site. An AI can check and fix these for you.
    8. 
        
  8. Accessibility. Run the automated scan. It will not catch everything, but the
        problems it does catch are usually quick to fix and genuinely shut people out.
    9. 
      

      
Turn on Agent-checkable only at the top of the checklist and most of this becomes

Using the page

      
Tick things off as you go. Your ticks are saved in your browser, so you can close the tab
      and come back — but they live in that browser, on that device, and nowhere
      else. Nothing is sent anywhere.

      
You can install it like an app: Chrome and Edge offer an Install app button in the
      page, and on an iPhone it is Share → Add to Home Screen. Installed, it works offline.

      
The labels on each check

agent-checkable

        
An AI assistant can settle this by itself, by looking at your live site.

        
agent + you

        
An AI can gather the facts, but the judgement is yours.

        
if applicable / if required

        
Only relevant to some sites. Read it, decide, tick it if it does not apply to you.

      

    


    

      
How this works with 

      
51 of the 73 checks are things an assistant can settle by fetching your site and reading
      what comes back — headers, status codes, the sitemap, the accessibility scan. Each one
      carries its own instructions, so you do not need to know what a security header is to get
      one checked.


      

        
 runs , which means it can use
        the checklist's own tools: it asks the page what to check next, does it, and writes the
        result back. You watch the list fill in as it goes. Nothing to copy, nothing to paste.

        
Keep the checklist tab in front of it while you ask — an assistant acts on the page it
        is looking at, so if you have switched tabs it may be reading something else.

      


      

        
 runs . It can read the whole
        checklist perfectly well — what it cannot do is write into your copy, because
        browsers keep each site's data to themselves and its browser is not yours. Ask it to
        audit your site and you get an excellent report and an empty checklist.

        
The prompt above closes that gap: it asks for the findings as a block of JSON, and the
        checklist's Save, load or share this run panel files every one against the right
        check, evidence included. One paste, once, at the end.

        
It also asks the assistant to check whether the page offered it tools, and if so to
        finish with a share link instead — same destination, less to copy. That costs nothing if
        it cannot, and saves you a step if it can.

      


      

        
Checking a site means making real requests to it and reading what comes back. An
        assistant that can only answer from what it already knows cannot do that, however capable
        it is at everything else — and one that can browse but chooses not to will give you a
        confident answer built on nothing.

        
So the list of apps that work is shorter than you would expect, and it is about
        plumbing rather than intelligence. Pick one of the ones above that has it.

      


      
What good looks like

      
Every result should arrive with evidence — the actual header it read, the status code it
      got, the number it measured. A check that fails does not get ticked: something that
      was looked at and found wanting is worse than something nobody has looked at, so it stays
      open with a red mark against it.


      
What to be sceptical about

      
An assistant saying "passed" is a claim, not proof. That is what the evidence is for. If a
      result arrives with vague evidence, or evidence that just repeats the check back at you,
      treat it as unchecked — and spot-check a couple of the ones that matter most to you.

      
It also cannot settle the 22 checks that need a person, and should not pretend otherwise.
      If it ticks off "proofread every page", it is making things up.

    


    

      
Something failed. Now what?

      
Most findings are fixed in the same place you built the site. The evidence line is the
      useful part — it is the specific, quotable fact that turns a vague worry into a task.

      
Copy the check and its evidence into whatever built your site — 
      or otherwise — and ask plainly:

      
My site fails this check: "Content-Security-Policy in enforcing mode". What
      was found: the live header is only frame-ancestors 'none', the rest of the policy is in a
      meta tag. Explain what that means in plain English, tell me whether it matters for my site,
      and if it does, make the change.

      
Asking it to explain first, and only then to fix, is deliberate. It gives you a chance to
      notice when the answer does not apply to your situation — and some findings genuinely do not
      matter for a small personal site. Deciding to skip something is a fine outcome. Not knowing
      it was there is not.

      
When it is fixed, ask the AI to re-run that one check rather than trusting that the change
      worked.

    


    

      
The part no AI can do for you

      
22 checks need a person, and they are not the leftovers — several are the ones most likely
      to embarrass you:

      
    
        
  • Read your own site. Every page, out loud if you can bear it. Spellcheck cannot
        catch a real word in the wrong place, and AI-written copy is fluent enough to hide it.
    • 
        
  • Send a message through every form, and confirm it arrives somewhere a human
        reads. Test the reply address too.
    • 
        
  • Use it on your phone, on mobile data, the way a stranger would.
    • 
        
  • Try it with the keyboard only — no mouse, just Tab and Enter. If you cannot
        reach something, neither can a lot of people.
    • 
        
  • Watch someone else use it for five minutes without helping them. This finds more
        than the rest of the list put together.
    • 
        
  • Check you can restore a backup, before you need to.
    • 
      

    


    

      
Words you will meet

      

        
robots.txt

        
A file that tells search engines what they may look at. A leftover setting here can
        hide your whole site from Google.

        
Sitemap

        
A list of your pages, for search engines. It should list the real addresses of pages
        that exist — not old ones that redirect.

        
Canonical URL

        
The one official address for a page, when several could reach it. Stops search engines
        treating copies as separate pages.

        
Redirect (301)

        
An instruction that a page has permanently moved. Needed when addresses change, so old
        links keep working.

        
404 / 500

        
"That page does not exist" and "something broke at our end". Both need a real page and
        the right signal to browsers and search engines.

        
HTTPS / TLS certificate

        
The padlock. It encrypts the connection, expires periodically, and should renew itself.

        
Security headers (CSP, HSTS)

        
Instructions your server sends with every page, limiting what can run on it and
        insisting on an encrypted connection. Invisible, and worth getting right.

        
Lighthouse

        
A free tool built into Chrome that scores speed, accessibility and basic good practice.
        Right-click a page → Inspect → Lighthouse.

        
Core Web Vitals

        
Three measurements of how fast a page feels: how quickly the main thing appears, how
        quickly it responds, and how much it jumps around while loading.

        
WCAG 2.2 AA

        
The accessibility standard most laws point at. "AA" is the level nearly everyone means.

        
DNS

        
The address book that connects your domain name to where your site actually lives.
        Changes here take time to spread, so do them before launch day.

        
SPF / DKIM / DMARC

        
Three DNS settings that prove email from your domain is really yours. Without them your
        form notifications land in spam.

        
alt text

        
A short description of an image, read aloud to people who cannot see it.

        
noindex

        
A tag telling search engines to skip a page. Useful deliberately, ruinous accidentally.
