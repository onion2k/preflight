How to Use Preflight

You have built a website — probably with a lot of help from an AI — and it works. Launching it is a different job from building it, and it is the part nobody shows you. This is what to check before you put it in front of people, in the order that matters.

Open the checklist

What the checklist actually is

73 things worth checking before a website goes live, grouped by the kind of problem they prevent.

None of it is about whether your site is good. It is about the specific, boring failures that are invisible from the inside and expensive from the outside: a page that tells Google not to list you, a contact form that quietly sends nothing, a broken link in the one place people click, a copyright year stuck in the past.

You will not need all 73. Plenty will not apply to you, and the checklist says so where that is likely. The point is to decide about each one rather than discover it later.

Start here if you have twenty minutes

Eight items are marked blocker. They are the ones that are hard to undo, or that do damage quietly while you think everything is fine. In rough order of how often they go wrong on a site built quickly:

  1. Placeholder text. Search your live site for words like lorem, TODO, example.com and Coming soon. AI-built sites are full of them, and they are invisible until someone else reads the page.
  2. The robots file. One line in a file called robots.txt can tell every search engine to ignore your entire site. It is the single most common launch mistake, and you cannot see it by looking at your pages.
  3. The 404 page. Type nonsense after your domain name. You should get a proper "not found" page with a way back — not a blank screen, and not your homepage pretending nothing is wrong.
  4. Secrets in the code. If you pasted an API key, password or token anywhere during building, assume it is public. Check, and change it if it was.
  5. The privacy page. If your site collects anything at all — a form, analytics, an embedded video — it needs a page saying so, honestly.
  6. Who owns the domain. It should be registered to you, with auto-renew on and a working email address on the account. Domains lapse silently.
  7. Security headers. A handful of settings that tell browsers how to protect people on your site. An AI can check and fix these for you.
  8. Accessibility. Run the automated scan. It will not catch everything, but the problems it does catch are usually quick to fix and genuinely shut people out.

Turn on Agent-checkable only at the top of the checklist and most of this becomes something you can hand to an AI. More on that below.

Using the page

Tick things off as you go. Your ticks are saved in your browser, so you can close the tab and come back — but they live in that browser, on that device, and nowhere else. Nothing is sent anywhere.

You can install it like an app: Chrome and Edge offer an Install app button in the page, and on an iPhone it is Share → Add to Home Screen. Installed, it works offline.

Getting an AI to do the mechanical half

51 of the 73 checks are things an AI can verify by fetching your site and reading what comes back. The checklist carries the exact instructions for each one, so you do not have to know what a security header is to get one checked.

The way that works best

Open the checklist in a browser whose AI assistant can act on the page you are looking at — Chrome or Edge with their built-in assistant, or Brave's Leo. Then ask it something like:

Use the tools on this page to check https://yoursite.com. Work through the agent-checkable items, record each result with the evidence, and tell me what failed.

It will work down the list, and its findings appear against the items with the actual evidence — the header it read, the status code it got, the number it measured. A finding that fails does not tick the box: a check that ran and did not pass is worse than one nobody has looked at, so it stays open and shows a red mark.

If your AI is somewhere else

An assistant in a different app — ChatGPT Desktop, or a chat window in another browser — keeps its own copy of the results, because browsers keep each site's data separate. Ask it for a share link when it finishes, open that link in your own browser, and its findings merge into your list. Anything you had already ticked yourself stays as you left it.

What to be sceptical about

An AI saying "passed" is a claim, not proof. That is why every result it records has to carry evidence. If a result has no evidence behind it, treat it as unchecked, and spot-check a couple of the ones that matter most to you.

Something failed. Now what?

Most findings are fixed in the same place you built the site. The evidence line is the useful part — it is the specific, quotable fact that turns a vague worry into a task.

Copy the check and its evidence into whatever built your site, and ask plainly:

My site fails this check: "Content-Security-Policy in enforcing mode". What was found: the live header is only frame-ancestors 'none', the rest of the policy is in a meta tag. Explain what that means in plain English, tell me whether it matters for my site, and if it does, make the change.

Asking it to explain first, and only then to fix, is deliberate. It gives you a chance to notice when the answer does not apply to your situation — and some findings genuinely do not matter for a small personal site. Deciding to skip something is a fine outcome. Not knowing it was there is not.

When it is fixed, ask the AI to re-run that one check rather than trusting that the change worked.

The part no AI can do for you

22 checks need a person, and they are not the leftovers — several are the ones most likely to embarrass you:

  • Read your own site. Every page, out loud if you can bear it. Spellcheck cannot catch a real word in the wrong place, and AI-written copy is fluent enough to hide it.
  • Send a message through every form, and confirm it arrives somewhere a human reads. Test the reply address too.
  • Use it on your phone, on mobile data, the way a stranger would.
  • Try it with the keyboard only — no mouse, just Tab and Enter. If you cannot reach something, neither can a lot of people.
  • Watch someone else use it for five minutes without helping them. This finds more than the rest of the list put together.
  • Check you can restore a backup, before you need to.

Words you will meet

robots.txt
A file that tells search engines what they may look at. A leftover setting here can hide your whole site from Google.
Sitemap
A list of your pages, for search engines. It should list the real addresses of pages that exist — not old ones that redirect.
Canonical URL
The one official address for a page, when several could reach it. Stops search engines treating copies as separate pages.
Redirect (301)
An instruction that a page has permanently moved. Needed when addresses change, so old links keep working.
404 / 500
"That page does not exist" and "something broke at our end". Both need a real page and the right signal to browsers and search engines.
HTTPS / TLS certificate
The padlock. It encrypts the connection, expires periodically, and should renew itself.
Security headers (CSP, HSTS)
Instructions your server sends with every page, limiting what can run on it and insisting on an encrypted connection. Invisible, and worth getting right.
Lighthouse
A free tool built into Chrome that scores speed, accessibility and basic good practice. Right-click a page → Inspect → Lighthouse.
Core Web Vitals
Three measurements of how fast a page feels: how quickly the main thing appears, how quickly it responds, and how much it jumps around while loading.
WCAG 2.2 AA
The accessibility standard most laws point at. "AA" is the level nearly everyone means.
DNS
The address book that connects your domain name to where your site actually lives. Changes here take time to spread, so do them before launch day.
SPF / DKIM / DMARC
Three DNS settings that prove email from your domain is really yours. Without them your form notifications land in spam.
alt text
A short description of an image, read aloud to people who cannot see it.
noindex
A tag telling search engines to skip a page. Useful deliberately, ruinous accidentally.

If you only do three things

  1. Search your live site for placeholder text.
  2. Visit yoursite.com/robots.txt and make sure it does not say Disallow: /.
  3. Send a message through your own contact form and check it arrives.

Then come back and do the rest properly.
